Excavation & Site Prep

What to Expect from Your Excavation & Site Prep Project

From permits to final grading — a step-by-step guide for Oregon Coast projects.

Gettis Bros Team

Whether you’re building a new home in Newport, putting in a septic system near Siletz, or grading a lot for a driveway in Lincoln City, excavation and site prep is the foundation of every successful construction project. Get it wrong, and everything built on top of it suffers. Get it right, and you’re set up for decades of stability.

Here on the Oregon Coast, excavation comes with unique challenges — wet soils, high water tables, steep slopes, and strict environmental regulations. This guide walks you through what to expect so there are no surprises when the equipment shows up.

When Do You Need Excavation?

Most property owners don’t think about excavation until a contractor tells them they need it. But site prep is required for more projects than people realize:

  • New home construction — foundation prep, grading, and utility trenching are day-one tasks on any new build
  • Driveway installation — proper grading and subbase compaction prevent sinking and washouts
  • Septic systems — perc tests, drain field layout, and tank placement all require precision excavation
  • Drainage correction — regrading, French drains, and swales to redirect water away from structures
  • Retaining walls and hardscaping — structural hardscape projects need excavated footings and properly compacted fill
  • Land clearing — removing stumps, brush, and debris before any construction begins

If your project involves changing the shape of the ground in any way, you need excavation work done correctly from the start.

Permitting Requirements in Lincoln County

Before a single bucket of dirt moves, permits and regulatory compliance come first. On the Oregon Coast, this isn’t optional — and skipping steps can result in fines, stop-work orders, or having to undo completed work.

Here’s what you’ll typically need:

  • Lincoln County grading permit — required for any significant earth-moving activity, especially on slopes or near waterways
  • Oregon DEQ 1200-C permit — for construction sites disturbing one acre or more, this covers stormwater discharge and sediment control
  • Erosion and sediment control plan — silt fences, straw wattles, and stabilization measures must be in place before excavation starts
  • Wetland and riparian setbacks — many coastal properties border protected wetlands or streams with mandatory buffer zones
  • Septic permits — DEQ approval for any new or replacement septic system, including required soil testing

A local contractor who’s done this work throughout Lincoln County knows the permitting process inside and out. We handle permit applications and inspections regularly in Newport, Toledo, Lincoln City, and surrounding communities, so projects don’t stall over paperwork.

The Step-by-Step Process

Every excavation project follows a similar sequence, though the scope and timeline vary based on the site and what you’re building. Here’s the general flow:

1. Site survey and assessment. A thorough evaluation of the property comes first. We look at existing grades, soil conditions, drainage patterns, buried utilities, and any environmental concerns. For larger projects, a professional land survey establishes benchmarks and property boundaries.

2. Clearing and demolition. Trees, stumps, brush, old structures, and debris get removed. On coastal properties, this often means working around protected vegetation and managing root systems in sandy or clay-heavy soil.

3. Rough grading. This is the heavy earthwork phase — cutting high spots, filling low spots, and shaping the land to the engineered plan. On sloped lots common in Newport and Depoe Bay, this stage takes the most time and equipment.

4. Drainage installation. Before final grading, we install drainage infrastructure — culverts, French drains, catch basins, and swales. Proper drainage is non-negotiable on the coast, where annual rainfall regularly exceeds 70 inches.

5. Utility trenching. Trenches for water, sewer, electrical, and communication lines get dug to spec. Depth and separation requirements vary by utility type and local code.

6. Compaction and fine grading. The final grade gets set and compacted to meet engineering specifications. Compaction testing confirms the soil can support the planned structure — whether that’s a foundation, a driveway, or a slab.

Soil Considerations on the Coast

Coastal soil is not like inland soil, and any excavation contractor working here needs to understand the difference. In Lincoln County, we encounter several soil types that each present unique challenges:

  • Heavy clay — common in Toledo and inland valleys. Clay holds water, expands when wet, and shrinks when dry. It requires careful compaction and often needs amended fill for structural applications.
  • Sandy loam — found closer to the shoreline and in areas like Gleneden Beach. Sandy soils drain fast but can lack the bearing capacity needed for foundations without proper compaction.
  • High water tables — many coastal lots have water within two to four feet of the surface, especially during winter. This affects foundation design, septic placement, and what kind of drainage you need.
  • Organic and peat layers — some low-lying lots near estuaries and wetlands have deep organic layers that cannot support structures. These soils often need to be removed and replaced with engineered fill.

We run into all of these conditions regularly across jobs in Newport, Waldport, Siletz, and Yachats. Understanding what’s under your property before excavation begins saves time, money, and headaches down the road.

Timeline Expectations

One of the most common questions we hear: “How long will this take?” The honest answer depends on the scope, but here are realistic ranges for typical projects:

  • Small residential (driveway grading, drainage fix) — 1 to 3 days of active work
  • Septic installation — 3 to 5 days including excavation, tank set, and drain field
  • Full lot prep for new construction — 1 to 3 weeks depending on lot size, slope, and soil conditions
  • Permitting timeline — plan for 2 to 6 weeks for permit approvals before work can start

Weather is always a factor on the coast. We schedule excavation work during drier windows when possible, but erosion control measures let us work safely through most conditions. The key is planning ahead — the earlier you start the permitting process, the sooner equipment can get on-site.

Why a Local Contractor Matters

Excavation isn’t a project where you want the low bidder from three counties away. The contractor who knows your terrain, your soil, and your local permitting office will save you more money than any discount.

That institutional knowledge means fewer change orders, fewer delays, and a finished product that holds up for the long haul.
