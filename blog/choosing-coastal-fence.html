Choosing the Right Fence for Coastal Weather

Wind, salt spray, and rain demand fencing built for the Oregon Coast.

By Gettis Bros Team

If you live along the Oregon Coast, you already know that the weather doesn’t go easy on anything. Fences are no exception. Between the constant salt spray, driving rain, and sustained winds that come off the Pacific, a fence that would last twenty years inland might start failing in five out here. We’ve been building and repairing fences across Lincoln County for decades, and the material and design choices you make upfront determine whether your fence holds or falls apart. Here’s what we tell every homeowner from Lincoln City to Waldport before a single post goes in the ground.

How Salt Spray and Moisture Accelerate Rot

Before comparing materials, it helps to understand what your fence is actually up against. Salt spray travels further inland than most people realize — properties a half-mile or more from the beach still catch enough airborne salt to corrode metal fasteners and accelerate wood decay. That salt pulls moisture into the wood grain, which feeds fungal growth year-round. Unlike eastern Oregon or the Willamette Valley, the coast never truly dries out. Relative humidity stays high even in summer, which means wood fencing stays damp for months at a time. That’s the perfect recipe for rot, especially where the posts meet the soil.

We’ve pulled out fence posts in Newport and Depoe Bay that looked solid above ground but were completely soft below grade — eaten from the inside by moisture and fungal decay that started within the first few years. The takeaway: material selection and proper installation aren’t optional on the coast. They’re the whole game.

Cedar vs. Pressure-Treated Wood for Coastal Conditions

Western red cedar is the traditional go-to for fencing in the Pacific Northwest, and for good reason. It contains natural oils (thujaplicins) that resist rot and insect damage without chemical treatment. Cedar weathers to a silver-gray that looks right at home on a coastal property, and it’s lighter than pressure-treated lumber, which makes installation easier. On the downside, cedar is softer — high winds can crack or split individual pickets — and the cost has climbed steadily over the past several years.

Pressure-treated wood (typically southern yellow pine or Douglas fir treated with copper-based preservatives) costs less per board foot and is structurally stronger than cedar. It handles impact and wind loads better. But here’s the catch: the treatment chemicals can react with salt air over time, and the metal fasteners used with pressure-treated lumber are especially vulnerable to corrosion near the coast. If you go this route, use stainless steel or hot-dipped galvanized fasteners exclusively — standard zinc-plated screws will rust through within two to three years.

Our recommendation for most Lincoln County fencing projects: cedar for the fence boards and rails, pressure-treated posts set deep in the ground. You get the natural rot resistance where it’s exposed and the structural strength where it matters most. Seal or stain the cedar within the first year of installation and reapply every two to three years.

Vinyl Fencing in Salt Air: Pros and Cons

Vinyl (PVC) fencing has obvious appeal on the coast: it doesn’t rot, doesn’t need staining, and salt spray won’t corrode it. For homeowners who want zero maintenance, vinyl is hard to beat. It cleans up with a hose, never needs paint, and won’t splinter.

That said, vinyl has real limitations in coastal environments:

  • Wind vulnerability: Solid vinyl panels act like sails in high winds. We’ve seen entire vinyl fence sections ripped off their posts during winter storms in Lincoln City and Depoe Bay.
  • UV degradation: Constant sun exposure (yes, even through overcast skies) can make vinyl brittle over the years, especially with cheaper grades.
  • Temperature swings: Vinyl expands and contracts more than wood. Loose-fitting rails and popping panels are common complaints after a few seasons.
  • Limited repair options: When a vinyl panel breaks, you typically replace the entire section rather than swapping a single board.

Vinyl works well for sheltered properties, privacy fencing between homes, and areas with lower wind exposure. For exposed coastal lots, especially hilltop properties or anything facing the ocean, wood or a wind-rated design is a better call. If you’re unsure, we’re happy to walk the property and give you an honest recommendation — just reach out for a free estimate.

Wind-Rated Fence Designs: Spacing, Post Depth, and Panel vs. Picket

The biggest mistake we see on coastal fences isn’t the wrong material — it’s the wrong design. A six-foot solid privacy fence with no gaps is essentially a wall for the wind to push against. Here’s how to build for wind:

  • Board-on-board or shadowbox designs allow wind to pass through while still providing visual privacy. Each board overlaps the gap between the boards behind it. You can’t see through it straight on, but air moves freely. This dramatically reduces wind load.
  • Picket fencing with spacing between boards (typically 1–2 inches) handles coastal gusts far better than solid panels. Even small gaps make a significant difference.
  • Shorter fences hold better. Going from six feet to five feet reduces the wind load by roughly 30%. If full privacy isn’t essential, a four-foot fence is remarkably more resilient.
  • Post depth matters enormously. We set posts a minimum of 36 inches deep on the coast — 42 inches or deeper for six-foot fences or exposed lots. Standard 24-inch post holes that work in sheltered areas will fail out here.
  • Post spacing at 6 feet on center instead of the common 8 feet gives each section more anchor points and shortens the span the wind can push against.

These aren’t upgrades — they’re baseline requirements for any fence between Lincoln City and Waldport. We factor them into every fencing installation we quote.

Post Anchoring in Sandy and Clay Coastal Soil

Soil along the Oregon Coast varies widely. Newport and Toledo tend toward heavy clay. Lincoln City and Gleneden Beach have sandier ground. Both present challenges for fence posts.

In sandy soil, posts need wider concrete footings to prevent leaning. We typically dig a wider hole (12–14 inches in diameter) and pour concrete around the post with a slight crown above grade so water sheds away from the base. Without concrete, sandy soil provides almost no lateral resistance — the first strong storm will push the fence over.

In clay soil, drainage is the issue. Clay holds water against the post, accelerating rot even in pressure-treated lumber. We add a few inches of gravel at the bottom of each post hole before setting the post and pouring concrete. This gives water somewhere to go instead of pooling around the base. In areas with a high water table, we sometimes use steel post anchors driven into the ground to keep the wood entirely above the wet zone.

Either way, never skip the concrete. Tamped dirt and gravel alone aren’t enough for coastal conditions. A properly set post in concrete can last 15–20 years even in salt air. A post set in dirt might lean within the first winter.

Maintenance Tips for Coastal Fences

Even the best-built fence needs attention on the coast. Here’s what we tell our customers after installation:

  • Rinse the fence with fresh water two to three times a year, especially after storm season. This washes off accumulated salt before it can do damage.
  • Reapply stain or sealant every two to three years on cedar or wood fencing. Use a product rated for marine or coastal environments — standard deck stain won’t hold up.
  • Inspect posts at the ground line every spring. Push against each post. Any give or wobble means the base is compromised and needs attention before the next storm season.
  • Clear vegetation away from the fence line. Ivy, blackberries, and moss trap moisture against the wood and speed up rot. Keep a 6-inch gap between plants and fence boards.
  • Replace damaged boards immediately. A missing picket changes the wind dynamics on the entire section and puts stress on the remaining boards.
  • Check metal fasteners for rust. If you see orange streaks running down the wood, the fasteners are corroding. Replace them with stainless steel before the joint fails.

A coastal fence that gets regular maintenance will outlast a neglected one by a decade or more. The work is minimal — a few hours twice a year keeps everything solid.
